    Coaching guide

    The same depth means different work on the way down and the way up. Each phase below gives what the muscles are doing, what this reference rep shows, what to look for, and the common faults with their cues. Select a heading to jump the model to that phase.

    Contraction isometricShoulder external rotation Elbow flexion Grip

    The bar sits on the upper trapezius, above the spine of the shoulder blade. The shoulder blades are pulled back and down to make a muscular shelf, the elbows point down under or slightly behind the bar, and the wrists stay straight with the bar in the heel of the hand. A full breath into the abdomen, held against a braced trunk wall, raises intra-abdominal pressure before the descent starts.

    Look for

    • Bar level and centred on the shelf
    • Wrists straight, knuckles up
    • Elbows pointing at the floor
    • Whole foot loaded, toes out about 20°

    Faults and cues

    • Soft upper back: “pull the bar into your traps”, “bend the bar”
    • Bar hanging on bent wrists: “bar in the heel of the hand”
    • Elbows flaring back because the grip is narrower than shoulder mobility allows: widen the grip
    • No brace: “breathe into the belt, then descend”

    Duration Peak bar speed Ground force

    Hips and knees break together and the bar travels straight down over midfoot. The prime movers lengthen while they work, so they brake the load (eccentric action). A muscle needs fewer active motor units to hold a given force while lengthening than while shortening, so estimated activation on the way down sits well below the drive at the same depth. The descent opens with a brief unweighting and finishes with a braking phase as the lifter slows the bar into the bottom.

    Length change from standing to the bottom position in this model: . Rectus femoris crosses both hip and knee, so it stays close to its standing length.

    Look for

    • Knees tracking over the second and third toes
    • Shin and trunk inclining together
    • Vertical bar path
    • Even speed into the bottom, with no free fall

    Faults and cues

    • Hips shoot back first: “knees and hips together”, “sit between your heels”
    • Knees cave in: “spread the floor”, “knees over toes”
    • Weight drifts to the toes: “whole foot”, “heels down”
    • Dropping fast and bouncing off soft tissue: “own the descent, two counts down”

    Transition Ground force peak Depth Lumbar curve

    The bar stops and reverses. Ground force peaks here because the system first decelerates and then accelerates upward, so the joint moments in the hole exceed the static values shown in most textbooks. The prime movers are at their longest and switch from lengthening to shortening through a brief near-isometric period, and stretched tendons and connective tissue return some of the energy stored during braking (the stretch-shortening cycle). Tension has to stay on: a relaxed bottom position, or a long pause, gives most of that stored energy away.

    Look for

    • Depth reached with the lower-back curve still present
    • Knees still over the toes, heels down
    • Chest and upper back still set against the bar

    Faults and cues

    • Relaxing in the hole: “stay tight and rebound, don’t rest”
    • Pelvis tucks under at the bottom: brace harder, stop just above the point where it tucks, check ankle dorsiflexion
    • Upper back rounds: “elbows down, chest up into the bar”

    Duration Mean bar speed Peak Sticking region

    The prime movers now shorten (concentric action). Activation is highest just after the reversal, and motor units fire faster in this phase than when lowering the same load. The first few centimetres are helped by the stretch-shortening rebound. Then comes the sticking region: in heavy reps, bar speed dips between roughly 8 and 22 cm above the bottom as knee-extensor leverage worsens and the rebound fades. Glute activity rises into this zone while rectus femoris and vastus lateralis activity falls. Once through it, bar speed climbs again.

    Look for

    • Hips and shoulders rising at the same rate, so trunk angle holds through the sticking region
    • Vertical bar path
    • Knees staying out

    Faults and cues

    • Hips rise first and the chest drops (good-morning squat): “drive your upper back into the bar”, “chest and hips up together”
    • Knees cave on the way up: “knees out”
    • Bar drifts forward: “push through midfoot”

    Ground force Contraction concentric, then isometric

    Hips and knees finish extending together. The bar is decelerating here, so ground force drops below system weight and the last few centimetres ride on momentum. Stand tall without leaning back, then breathe and re-brace before the next rep.

    Look for

    • Hips and knees locking out together
    • Neutral lower back at the top

    Faults and cues

    • Leaning back to finish: “stand tall, ribs down”
    • Losing the brace between reps: “breathe and brace every rep”

    What is modelled

    Anatomy. Every bone and muscle is an anatomical surface from BodyParts3D, scaled to a 1.78 m lifter. Leg muscles follow the paths of the Rajagopal full-body OpenSim model with the Lai et al. deep-flexion update, so they stretch, shorten and wrap with the motion. Trunk muscles bend with the lumbar spine. The left side mirrors the right.

    Upper back, arms and grip. The shoulder blades are retracted about 12°. The arm pose was solved under joint limits in ISB conventions (Wu et al. 2005): shoulder external rotation (the top of the normal range), elevation , elbow flexion , and wrists within of neutral. The hands are about apart in a full grip, thumb around the bar. The deltoid and pectoralis major were re-shaped from their rest anatomy with dual-quaternion skinning so they follow the 115° humeral rotation without collapsing.

    Path and balance. The feet are fixed with heels 32 cm apart and toes out 20°. Knee and ankle angles are prescribed. At every depth the trunk is rotated about the hips so the bar stays directly over midfoot, which makes the bar path vertical. The centre of pressure then follows the combined centre of mass of lifter and bar, moving from to of foot length from the heel.

    Timed rep. The rep timing is shaped on published barbell data: a controlled descent, a touch-and-go reversal, and a drive at mean velocity, which the full-squat velocity table places at about 80–85% of 1RM. It includes a mild sticking region where the literature locates it. It is a reference rep, not a recording.

    Kinetics. Net joint moments come from body and bar weight using de Leva (1996) segment parameters for an 85 kg lifter with a 100 kg bar, scaled at each instant by the vertical acceleration of the combined centre of mass. Segment angular accelerations and horizontal forces are left out; they are small in a controlled rep but not in a bouncy one. Hip, knee and ankle moments are per leg. L5/S1 carries the whole trunk. The floor can push each foot inward and twist it, the way lifters “spread the floor”, within friction limits.

    Muscle activation. Estimated, not measured. Each leg and trunk muscle scales with the joint moment it works against, calibrated to published surface EMG from loaded back squats (Contreras et al. 2015, Caterisano et al. 2002, Hammond et al. 2016, van den Tillaar et al. 2019). A contraction-mode factor then separates the phases: lengthening muscles need about 0.62 of the activation they need when shortening at the same moment, near-isometric muscles about 0.8–0.95, and both phases meet at the reversal. Shoulder, arm and neck values are typical holding levels scaled by the bar’s effective weight.

    Muscle length. Musculotendon path length relative to standing, from the model’s muscle paths. Tendons stretch, so fibre behaviour can differ from the whole unit, most of all in the calf muscles. Short deep hip rotators are not reported because their paths are too short to measure reliably.

    What would make it measured

    • Motion capture, either markers or OpenCap video, for joint angles
    • Force plates for ground force and centre of pressure
    • A linear position transducer or velocity tracker for bar speed
    • Surface EMG for activation, normalised to maximal contractions

    Limits

    • One body size and one rep. Proportions change trunk lean and knee travel.
    • No skin or fat layer. Muscles can pass through each other where soft tissue would compress in reality.
    • The head stays fixed to the trunk, so the gaze drops as the trunk leans.
    • Fast or bouncy reps add inertial terms this model leaves out.
